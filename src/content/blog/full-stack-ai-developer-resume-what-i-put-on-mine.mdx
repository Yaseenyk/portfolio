I rewrote my resume recently, and the process taught me more than any template did. The hard part was not the formatting. It was deciding what the page should claim I am, and making every line support that claim.

This post is how my resume is built now, what I put on it, what I cut, and why. Both versions are public, so you can compare what I say with what is on the page: the designed one is the [main resume PDF](/Resume.pdf) and the plain one is the [ATS-safe version](/Resume-ATS.pdf).

## Start with the role, not your history

The first line of a resume answers one question: what job is this person applying for. Mine says Full Stack AI Developer, then the stack (MERN, TypeScript, Next.js), then the proof area (LLM apps, RAG and AI agents in production). Someone who reads only that line knows where I fit.

A generic developer title is accurate and tells the reader nothing about which of your years matter. If you are moving from one specialty to another, the headline is where you make that move visible.

## The summary: three jobs in four lines

The summary does three things and nothing else.

1. **Names the stack and the years.** Five years of production web work in React, Next.js, Node.js, TypeScript and MongoDB or PostgreSQL.
2. **Says what you do now.** Since 2025 I build the AI tooling layer: a hybrid RAG system with measured retrieval quality, an MCP server and an AI code-review service.
3. **States what you are open to**, including where. Mine lists Hyderabad, Pune, Mumbai, Bangalore or remote in India, so a recruiter does not have to guess whether I will relocate.

It does not contain adjectives like "passionate" or "results-driven". Nobody can check those, so they carry no information.

## Bullets: what, with what, and a result

Each experience bullet follows one shape: what I built, the technology, and the outcome.

Here are two of mine, in plain form.

- At a previous job I made dashboards 1.5 to 2x faster by profiling slow MongoDB aggregation pipelines, adding a caching layer and lazy-loading heavy panels.
- In my current role I built a hybrid RAG system with an evaluation harness. The baseline was a Recall@5 of 0.95 and an MRR of 0.83, and the harness fails when quality drops below set thresholds. I wrote up how that works in [hybrid RAG for a codebase](/blog/hybrid-rag-for-a-codebase-bm25-vectors-reranker/).

Both have a number, and both numbers are mine. That is the test I apply: if an interviewer asks "how did you measure that", I can answer in a minute. A bullet with no number is fine when the work was real but not measured. What I will not do is invent a figure to fill the slot, because the follow-up question exposes it immediately.

## Bold the keywords, but only the ones that are true

In the designed version I bold the stack words inside the sentences: React, Next.js, TypeScript, RAG, MCP and so on. It is not decoration. A person skimming a resume is looking for a handful of terms, and bold lets the eye find them without reading every line.

Two rules keep it honest:

- Only bold technologies I have actually shipped with. If I have only read about it, it does not get emphasis.
- Do not bold everything. When half the page is bold, none of it is.

I also keep a short "Core" strip of the main skills near the top, because skills spread across a long list at the bottom are easy to miss.

## Two versions on purpose

A resume has two readers: a person and software. They want different things.

**The designed version** has a clearer hierarchy, an accent colour and bolded keywords. It runs to two pages and it is what I send when a human is reading.

**The plain version** is one page, single column, with standard headings: Summary, Experience, Selected projects, Skills and Education. No tables, no icons, no graphics, and the text is real text, not an image. Application tracking systems that parse a resume into fields handle that layout predictably. A fancy layout can scramble how the sections are read.

Same facts in both. The plain one just drops the styling.

## What I cut

- **A long skills dump.** I keep the skills I would be comfortable being interviewed on, grouped by kind, and drop the ones I touched once.
- **Duties without outcomes.** "Responsible for the dashboard" says nothing. "Made the dashboard faster by changing the query" does.
- **A photo, a full address and a skills bar chart.** None of them helps a hiring decision.
- **Anything I could not explain.** If I could not talk about a line for two minutes, it came off.

## Match the job description without lying

For each application I check the description for the stack words and make sure the ones I genuinely use are visible, in the summary or the bullets. I do not add a technology I have not used to match a posting. Beyond the ethics, it fails in the interview, where the first technical question lands on exactly that word.

## A checklist you can copy

1. Headline: the role, the stack, the proof area.
2. Summary: stack and years, what you do now, where you are open to work.
3. Bullets: what, with what, and a result you can defend.
4. Bold only the true keywords, sparingly.
5. Keep a plain single-column version for application systems.
6. Cut anything you could not explain for two minutes.

## FAQ

### What should a full stack developer resume include?

A headline naming the role and stack, a short summary, experience bullets with what you built and a result, selected projects, grouped skills and education. Keep it to one or two pages.

### How is an AI engineer resume different?

It should show the AI work with evidence: what you built (RAG, agents, evals), the stack, and a measured outcome. Retrieval quality numbers or latency figures carry more weight than a list of model names.

### Should I use a fancy template or a plain one?

Use both. A designed version for people and a plain single-column version for application systems, with identical facts.

### Do I bold keywords in a resume?

Yes, sparingly, and only for technologies you have genuinely shipped with. Bold helps a skimming reader find the stack in seconds.

### How long should a developer resume be?

One page for the plain version and up to two for the designed one. Cut what you could not explain in an interview.
